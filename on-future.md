# 关于未来



人们谈论 AI 超越人类时，常常从能力的增长直接跳到一个无限丰富的未来。我更想知道：当 AI 和文明能力继续增长以后，它们究竟会做什么？人类的医学、教育、娱乐以及各种现实需求，可以解释相当多近期用途，却不能直接说明更遥远的未来。造出越来越强的计算机之后，世界上还有多少值得计算的事情？



推测这样的未来，我更愿意从演化出发。能够维持、复制和扩张的系统，更有机会留下后继。研究、制造和信息活动都应放在这个过程中理解，而不能只给一个孤立的超级智能安排兴趣爱好。即使一部分活动的收益下降，也不意味着文明会集体停下来等待。不利用的能源未必能够保存，其他扩张者也不会一起休眠；等待宇宙变冷再计算，必须抵得过今天研究和行动可能带来的收益。



但扩张不等于不断产生新的层级。同一套成熟技术可以复制到无数恒星系，而不引入多少根本不同的活动。所谓 Dyson swarm，未必是布满建筑、地形和生物的巨大世界，也可能主要由采能、计算、制造和散热设施组成。今天让世界丰富的许多物质差异，来自生物身体及其生存方式；如果未来越来越多的功能可以由通用计算与自动化基础设施承担，这些差异是否还会同样重要，并不清楚。未来可以在规模上极其宏伟，在物质形态上却相当单调。



不同领域能够怎样利用更强的智能，也可能很不一样。有些能力偏向“定理化”：目标短而明确，评价可以在计算机内部完成，并且一个一般结论能够取代大量逐例搜索。数学和算法是最明显的例子。另一些能力则更像下棋中的巨大 matcher：底层规则可以很短，实际的高水平判断却可能依赖一个庞大而难以进一步压缩的函数。科学和工程中也可能同时存在这两种成分。知道微观物理规律，并不保证某个复杂宏观任务一定存在短的有效理论。



这也是廉价数学证明能否真正改变其他科学的关键。如果能够从基本物理出发找到简单而可靠的有效模型，并证明现实尺度下的误差界，那么原本需要反复实验或高保真仿真的巨大设计空间，可以先在数学模型内部探索。一次定理可能覆盖无数实例。但如果寻找证明比直接仿真更难，或者所谓“有效理论”本身几乎和被压缩的系统一样复杂，那么证明变便宜也没有带来根本突破。真正重要的不是形式上能否写成定理，而是现实中有多少现象存在短而可靠的中间层结构。



数学因此有两种需要分开讨论的未来地位。第一种是数学对其他领域的影响力。这里完全可能出现递减：容易利用的短规律和高杠杆数学结构不断被发现以后，技术前沿越来越依赖大规模仿真、搜索和训练得到的 matcher。深刻的新数学仍然不断产生，却越来越少直接改变材料、机器或日常技术。数学的成果越来越深，而它在整体技术能力中的作用却越来越稀薄，甚至相当一部分数学越来越像一种高度发展的内部探索。



第二种却是数学研究本身在未来认知活动中占多大比例。这没有理由跟前一种占比一起下降。恰恰相反，数学已有大量简明、明确而且几乎不会耗尽的目标，而且不依赖具体的现实需求。一个猜想解决后，新的问题往往随之出现；即使不知道还该研究什么，也至少可以研究 Busy Beaver 这样定义极短、困难却不断延伸的问题。算法研究在很大程度上也属于这片空间。其他领域则未必拥有同样稳定的任务来源：工程目标可能饱和，实验科学可能被现实反馈拖慢，很多复杂领域甚至越来越难说明到底什么才是一个简明而值得追求的下一步目标。因此，即使数学对世界的实际影响越来越小，数学研究本身仍然可能吸收很大比例的智能和算力。这两个比例必须分别判断。



然而，任务不会耗尽，不等于聪明的办法不会越来越昂贵。即使新的压缩和统一持续出现，理论本身也可能越来越长，发现它们的成本也越来越高。一个庞大的理论可以压缩更庞大的计算，却未必还具有今天那些短概念突然打开大片世界的力量。数学可能一直有进展，但单位投入换来的结构性收获不断下降，最终越来越接近费大力气推进有限的计算事实。它提供了明确的纵深，却没有保证这种纵深始终同样有趣。



物质上的单调，也不意味着内部体验必然单调。我的梦有时比现实有趣，未来的计算系统或许能够承载远比这些梦丰富的体验。信息与注意力的竞争也不只是瓜分资源，它可以伴随文化生产。然而，无数可生成的世界并不会自动成为无数值得运行的世界。如果缺少强有力的选择机制，所谓丰富性可能退化成对程序、自动机、神经网络和游戏规则的随机采样或枚举。更复杂、更陌生，并不自动等于更有趣。



历史或许能够提供这种选择。未来的文明可能继续经营由自身历史和文化生长出来的世界，不同起源的文明则可能沿着完全不同的路径发展。假如相关模拟能够实现，未来那些黑乎乎的计算设施里，也许运行着历史的重建、分支，以及建立在这些历史之上的创作。但这又带来一个严肃问题：为什么值得重建？如果模拟中的主体确实能够受苦，重演一段历史也可能意味着重新制造其中的苦难。历史给出了偏爱的理由，却没有自动给出这样做的正当性。



我仍然希望未来出现真正不同的新层级，而不只是把既有结构做大。这使基本物理成为一个重要悬念。快速发展的 AI 或未来文明或许能建造远超今天规模的加速器，探索新的物理条件。但发现新粒子，并不等于获得新的世界。真正重要的是，新自由度能否形成长期稳定、可控制、可组合的结构，继而产生新的材料和组织方式，而不只是短暂出现后衰变。



这种期待还必须与天文观察联系起来。如果新的丰富结构能够在自然条件下大量形成，就要问它们是否会在宇宙其他地方留下可辨认的痕迹。它们的丰度、相互作用和形成过程，都受到观察的约束。反过来，天然稀少或需要人工条件才能形成的结构，仍可能具有巨大的技术意义。因此，真正的未知不只是“还有多少新物理”，而是“还有多少新的物理能够支撑丰富的稳定结构”。



这些考虑让我怀疑，我们可能正活在一个异常有趣的时间：生物、社会和技术的多种层级同时存在，短而有力的发现仍能改变大片世界，而文明未来的形态尚未收敛。未来可能更强大，却未必不断经历这样的转折。当然，也可能仍有许多新的层级等待出现；只是目前，我们还看不见一个保证它们会不断出现的机制。


# On future



When people discuss AI surpassing humanity, they often move directly from increasing capability to an endlessly rich future. I am more interested in another question: as AI and civilization become more capable, what will they actually do? Human medicine, education, entertainment, and other practical needs explain many near-term applications, but they do not directly tell us what a much more distant future would contain. After building ever more powerful computers, how much will remain worth computing?



I prefer to approach such a future through evolution. Systems that maintain, reproduce, and expand themselves have more opportunities to leave successors. Research, manufacturing, and informational activity should be understood within this process, rather than treated as hobbies assigned to an isolated superintelligence. Declining returns in some activities would not mean that civilization collectively stops and waits. Unused energy may not remain available, and other expanding systems will not necessarily hibernate alongside it. Waiting for a colder universe must outweigh the possible benefits of research and action today.



But expansion does not imply the continuing emergence of new levels of organization. The same mature technology could spread across countless star systems without introducing many fundamentally different activities. A Dyson swarm need not be an immense world filled with buildings, landscapes, and organisms. It might consist mainly of energy collectors, computing systems, manufacturing facilities, and radiators. Much of the material diversity that makes our world rich comes from biological bodies and their ways of living. If more functions can eventually be carried by general-purpose computation and automated infrastructure, it is unclear whether all of those differences would remain equally important. The future could be magnificent in scale yet materially monotonous.



Different fields may also benefit from greater intelligence in fundamentally different ways. Some abilities are relatively theorem-like: their goals are short and explicit, evaluation can take place entirely inside a computer, and one general result can replace enormous amounts of case-by-case search. Mathematics and algorithms are the clearest examples. Other abilities may resemble the enormous matchers found in games such as chess: the underlying rules can be short while high-level judgment is represented by a large function with no known drastic compression. Science and engineering may contain both kinds of ability. Knowing the microscopic laws does not guarantee that every difficult macroscopic task admits a short effective theory.



This distinction is crucial to whether cheap mathematical proof could transform the other sciences. If simple and reliable effective models can be derived from fundamental physics, together with quantitative error bounds at realistic scales, then enormous design spaces that currently require repeated experiments or high-fidelity simulations could first be explored inside mathematics. A single theorem might cover an immense family of cases. But if finding the proof is harder than direct simulation, or if the “effective theory” is nearly as complicated as the system it replaces, then cheap proof brings no fundamental breakthrough. The important question is not whether a phenomenon can formally be written as a theorem, but how much of reality possesses short and reliable intermediate structure.



There are therefore two quite different future roles for mathematics, and they should not be conflated. The first is the influence of mathematics on other fields. That influence could plausibly decline. Once the easiest short laws and the highest-leverage mathematical structures have been extracted, the technological frontier may depend increasingly on large simulations, searches, and trained matchers. Deep new mathematics could continue to appear while directly changing fewer materials, machines, or everyday technologies. Mathematics might grow deeper while becoming thinner as a source of practical leverage, with an increasing fraction of it resembling highly developed internal exploration.



The second question is what share of future cognitive activity will itself consist of mathematical research. There is no reason for this share to move in the same direction. Mathematics already contains a vast supply of concise, explicit problems that are unlikely ever to be exhausted, and these problems do not depend on any particular practical demand. Solving one conjecture often exposes several others. Even when we no longer know what else to investigate, there remain problems such as Busy Beaver: extremely short to define, yet offering an indefinitely extending frontier of difficulty. Algorithmic research largely belongs to the same space. Other fields may not possess such a reliable supply of tasks. Engineering objectives can saturate, experimental science can become bottlenecked by contact with reality, and in many complicated domains it may become increasingly unclear what the next simple and worthwhile objective even is. Mathematics could therefore absorb a large fraction of intelligence and computation even while its practical influence on the rest of civilization becomes smaller. These two fractions have to be estimated separately.



An inexhaustible supply of tasks, however, does not mean that clever approaches will remain cheap. Even if new compressions and unifications keep appearing, the theories themselves may grow longer, and finding them may become increasingly expensive. A vast theory can compress an even vaster computation without retaining the leverage of a short concept that suddenly opens up an entire field. Mathematics might continue advancing while delivering less structural insight per unit of effort, increasingly resembling the laborious extension of finite computational knowledge. It offers a definite depth to explore, but no guarantee that this depth will remain equally interesting.



Material monotony need not imply monotonous experience. My dreams are sometimes more interesting than reality, and future computing systems might support experiences far richer than those dreams. Competition for information processing and attention can also produce culture rather than merely redistribute resources. Yet countless possible worlds do not automatically become countless worlds worth running. Without a strong selection mechanism, this apparent richness could reduce to random sampling or enumeration of programs, automata, neural networks, and game rules. Greater complexity and strangeness do not automatically mean greater interest.



History might provide such a selection mechanism. Future civilizations may continue developing worlds that grow out of their own histories and cultures, while civilizations of different origins may follow entirely different paths. If the relevant simulations are feasible, the dark computing installations of the future might contain reconstructions of history, alternative branches, and creations built upon them. But this raises a serious question: why would reconstruction be worthwhile? If the simulated subjects can genuinely suffer, replaying a history may mean recreating its suffering. History provides a reason for attachment, but not an automatic justification for acting on it.



I still hope that genuinely different levels will emerge, rather than merely larger versions of existing structures. This makes fundamental physics an important uncertainty. Rapidly developing AI or future civilizations might build accelerators far beyond today’s scale and explore new physical conditions. But discovering new particles is not the same as acquiring a new world. What matters is whether the new degrees of freedom can form long-lived, controllable, composable structures, enabling new materials and forms of organization rather than briefly appearing and decaying.



This expectation must also be connected to astronomical observation. If rich new structures can form abundantly under natural conditions, we should ask whether they would leave recognizable traces elsewhere in the universe. Their abundance, interactions, and formation processes are constrained by observation. Conversely, structures that are naturally rare or require artificial conditions could still have enormous technological significance. The crucial unknown is therefore not simply how much new physics remains, but how much of it can support a rich variety of stable structures.



These considerations make me suspect that we may be living at an unusually interesting time. Biological, social, and technological levels coexist; short, powerful discoveries can still transform large parts of the world; and civilization has not yet settled into its future form. The future may be far more capable without continually passing through transitions like these. There may, of course, be many further levels waiting to emerge; at present, however, we cannot see any mechanism that guarantees their continued appearance.
